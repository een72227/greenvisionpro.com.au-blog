## Summary

Weather sealing addresses the gaps and cracks in a home's building envelope that allow
conditioned air to escape and outside air to enter. Because heating and cooling systems have to
work harder to compensate for this air exchange, sealing these gaps can reduce the energy needed
to maintain a comfortable indoor temperature — though the impact varies by property.

## Where homes commonly lose air

- **External doors**, particularly gaps underneath and around the frame
- **Window frames and seals**, especially in older properties
- **Exhaust fans and vents** without dampers, which can allow air exchange even when not in use
- **Skirting boards and floorboards**, particularly in homes with suspended timber floors
- **Gaps around pipework, cabling and wall penetrations**

Older Victorian homes, in particular, were often built without the level of air sealing expected
in modern construction, which can mean more opportunities for draughts.

## Why sealing matters for heating and cooling costs

Heating and cooling systems are designed to maintain a set indoor temperature. When outside air
continually enters through gaps, the system has to work harder to offset that exchange. Sealing
these gaps doesn't change how efficient your heater or air conditioner is, but it can reduce how
much work that system has to do to maintain comfort.

## What a weather sealing assessment typically covers

A professional assessment usually involves checking common draught points throughout the home,
identifying which gaps are contributing most to air leakage, and recommending an appropriate
sealing approach for each — since not every gap should be sealed the same way. For example,
exhaust fans and some vents need to retain functional airflow for ventilation and moisture
control, even while draughts elsewhere are minimised.

## Balancing sealing with ventilation

Sealing a home too aggressively without maintaining adequate ventilation can contribute to
moisture build-up and indoor air quality issues. A considered approach targets unwanted draughts
while preserving the ventilation a home needs — this is one reason professional assessment is
often recommended over a purely DIY approach for whole-home sealing.

## Common weather sealing products

- Door seals and draught stoppers for external and internal doors
- Window seals and weatherstripping
- Exhaust fan draught stoppers with self-sealing dampers
- Gap sealing for skirting boards and floor penetrations
